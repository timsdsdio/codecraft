CodeCraft turns a running React app into a building you can walk around inside.

## The idea in one paragraph

The app's [[Screen]] lies face-up as the roof. Under it, each [[Floors|floor]] holds one level of the component tree: the root at the bottom, its children on the floor above, and so on up to the screen. Every component is a [[Logic Casing]] standing directly beneath the part of the screen it draws. Props flow **up** through [[Pipe|pipes]] from each casing to its children's casings. A [[Display Link]] carries each component's output the rest of the way up to its patch of screen. Clicks and keypresses flow **down** [[Event Wire|event wires]] to the component that owns the state, then a [[Render|render]] climbs back up.

## Why it's shaped like this

**The building is the component tree, and the roof is what that tree draws.** Standing on a row of the list, the machine making that row is directly under your feet. Its parent is one floor further down, under a bigger patch of screen, and so on down to the root.

**A child's footprint is inside its parent's.** A component's DOM box sits inside its parent's box, so each floor's machines sit inside the floor below's. Nothing overlaps, because siblings at the same depth don't overlap on screen.

**Deeper means closer to the source.** State lives in [[State Tank|tanks]], and in most apps it lives near the root. So crouching moves you toward the truth, and jumping moves you toward what the user sees. Data rises, events sink.

## The two tricks

1. **Scrolling is clipping, not physics.** A scroll area becomes a [[Conveyor Belt]] on its owner's floor. Everything inside the scroll area rides along with it, and anything that slides out of view is cut off by a [[Fold]]: the scroll area's footprint, extended from its floor up to the screen. That's the "higher-dimensional" scroll. Machines aren't stacked or hidden; they pass through a seam and stop being drawn.
2. **The app never knows it's being watched.** The real app runs unmodified in a frame laid flat as the screen. A [[Probe]] attached the way React DevTools attaches reads the component tree after every commit. The game builds the factory from what it reads ([[World Layout]]).

## A click, end to end

<SceneFigure name="event" float="right" width={320} caption="A click on a todo row: the signal runs down the red wires to App, then the render climbs back up." />

You click the checkbox on "Write the wiki". On the screen it ticks immediately, because the app is real. Underneath, the factory replays what happened ([[Update Replay]]):

1. A red pulse leaves the row's casing and runs down its `onToggle` [[Event Wire|wire]], past `TodoList`, to `App`, the casing that created the function.
2. `App`'s `todos` tank sloshes: its state changed.
3. `App`'s cog spins: it rendered. Its pipe to `TodoList` carries a new array up.
4. `TodoList` renders. Its piston pushes a new `todo` object up the third arm only. The other todos are the same objects as before, so nothing travels up their arms.
5. Every row's cog still spins, because nothing in this app is memoized and React re-runs them all. Only the third row's display link changes anything on the screen.

You watch React's one-way data flow happen, at a scale where you can walk around it.

## Scope

The aim is a good visualizer for **one** small app (the [[Todo App]]), then iteration. It doesn't need to handle every React app. What it must not do is require changes to the app's code.
